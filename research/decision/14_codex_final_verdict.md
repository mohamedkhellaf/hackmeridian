# Stage 2 — final independent verdict

## 1. Verdict

**OVERTURN — build Heirloom as a narrowly scoped inactivity-recovery feature for a classic Stellar account. Keep Loop as the backup.**

**Assumption — recommendation:** The next hour should produce a hosted, disposable-account Heirloom demonstration: save a recovery transaction, reject it early, reset its countdown with owner activity, recover after silence, and invalidate a separate recovery artifact by revocation. Exclude the two-stage low-weight heir, a policy server, legal-inheritance claims, arbitrary existing multisig accounts, and unsupported claims about every Soroban token. End the successful recovery demonstration with an heir-authorized payment so the benefit is tangible.

**Verified — decisive findings:** The group's final Second Thought design fixes the original parent's direct-payment bypass, but its advertised security boundary remains false. On fresh testnet accounts I independently demonstrated that **either the guardian or policy key can spend the parent's XLM on fees**, and the policy key can cancel pending payments and exhaust the parent's sequence number. More seriously, the recommended break-glass procedure first fails when all three keys sign, then **grants both delegates unilateral spending power** when the surplus signature is removed. Full results and transaction links are in §3. These are findings about the proposed configuration, not allegations about deployed application code.

**Assumption — why Heirloom is better now:** The remaining build window favors the smaller, directly testable recovery flow over a consumer-security product whose central permission model needs correction, whose policy service is unimplemented in the inspected scaffold, and whose demonstration presently overstates what its tests establish. A more narrowly worded Second Thought could be a legitimate prototype, but it loses much of the differentiator used to rank it first. Fixing a recovery payload is easy; obtaining confidence in the whole authorization and payment lifecycle is the larger task.

**Verified — what changed my mind about the evidence:** The reveal supplied working spikes for both finalists, a real redesign of Second Thought, and the specific Liana competitor. I reran Heirloom and Second Thought's integrated spike successfully. [Liana's own repository](https://github.com/wizardsardine/liana) confirms that inactivity-timelocked recovery already exists on Bitcoin. **Assumption:** That reduces Heirloom's innovation score, and I also reduce its user-value score because no direct customer validation was supplied. The positive Second Thought evidence improves my view of its basic feasibility; the new adversarial tests defeat the broader security conclusions. My final pick remains my blind pick for narrower, better-tested reasons, not because the prior vote was unanimous or because I must defend my first answer.

**Verified — additional single-party powers:** P has the same weight and account permissions as G. Both can repeatedly reclaim eligible balances, bump M's sequence, and authorize M-funded fees. For an account that itself issues assets, the Low-category trustline-authorization operations also matter. These powers are account-wide, not limited to balances created by Second Thought. **Assumption:** A UI that always chooses guardian-sourced undo cannot constrain a malicious signer constructing a different transaction.

**Verified — maximum-sequence nuance:** A maximum bump prevents normal M-sourced transactions and invalidates an M-sourced pre-signed recovery with a smaller sequence. It does not prevent M from being an operation source in an adequately authorized channel transaction, as my live test shows. **Assumption:** A compromised policy service can therefore disable both the planned normal UI and its pre-signed hatch; M+G can still rescue through a channel, but the specified UI does not implement this. Repeated no-op bumps can also prevent the inactivity condition from maturing. The hatch is not protection from an actively malicious low-threshold signer.

**Verified — break-glass root cause:** Raising M to weight 3 does not revoke P or G. Setting Medium and High to 1 makes either retained weight-1 delegate sufficient. The correct recovery goal must be specified as a post-state, not a copied transaction. **Assumption — repair if Second Thought is pursued later:** Atomically remove both delegate signers and restore suitable owner-only thresholds, sign with a sufficient non-surplus set, verify the complete resulting account state, and retest spending by each former signer. Retaining thresholds 3 with M weight 3 is another possible owner-controlled post-state if low authority is addressed. Neither change makes a same-account inactivity hatch resistant to malicious sequence bumps; disclose that limit or redesign authorization.

### 3.3 Claimable-balance and policy edge cases

1. **Verified:** The proposed sender predicate is unconditional. At or after T, both sender and recipient may be eligible; the first successful claim consumes the balance. **Assumption:** Describe T as the earliest collection time, not payment finality or automatic settlement. A transaction must actually be submitted. At the deadline, the guardian has no priority over the recipient.
2. **Verified:** Classic-asset claims require an appropriate trustline, authorization and capacity; creation does not guarantee eventual receivability. **Assumption:** Test missing trustline, full trustline, lost authorization, recipient not yet created, already claimed, and simultaneous claim/reclaim. Do not label every claim failure “too early.” Use a self-issued asset identified by both code and issuer and label it valueless testnet money.
3. **Verified:** Issuer controls can freeze a receivable trustline and, where the relevant clawback flag is enabled, burn a claimable balance. **Could-not-verify:** The actual mainnet Circle asset's current flag configuration was not inspected. **Assumption:** State conditional issuer powers; neither promise issuer-free stablecoin safety nor imply Circle has every possible power. XLM-only spikes do not exercise this trust model.
4. **Assumption — deterministic inference from the specified flow:** Clicking “Approve now” after funding a claimable balance must not simply send an additional Payment. That leaves the original balance claimable and risks paying twice. Atomically reclaim the original balance and pay its intended recipient with M+G authorization; if the recipient already collected, the transaction must fail as a whole. Alternatively, define instant approval solely as a pre-send action. Existing spikes test neither lifecycle.
5. **Verified — predicate semantics:** An absolute T is measured against ledger time, not time since submission. **Assumption — policy flaw:** Checking `T >= server_now + window` and merely requiring some timebounds permits a signed transaction with a long validity period to be held until T is close or past, then submitted without a fresh 24-hour on-ledger window. Require a tightly bounded submission interval with a compatible T, or use a strictly validated relative-time predicate. Client countdowns are informational.
6. **Assumption — policy validation requirements:** Enforce the intended network, transaction/envelope kind, expected account and signer configuration, allowed asset, operation count, bounded fee and validity, exact distinct claimants and predicate structure, and intended operation sources. Do not silently normalize a malicious predicate to a friendly display. An operation-type whitelist alone is insufficient. Reject unsupported fee-bump envelopes at this API or fully validate the inner transaction. The server's own fee cap does not constrain G signing fee bumps elsewhere.
7. **Assumption — allowlist limitation:** “Family only” is an off-chain assertion, not a property validators can establish. A compromised family account, forwarding by a persuaded family member, a guardian deceived into immediate approval, or an attacker willing to wait can still defeat the safety story. Treat it as a reduction in one attack path, not a proof of scam prevention.
